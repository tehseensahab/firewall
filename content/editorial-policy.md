These are the standards we hold our writing to. They apply to every article, including guest contributions.

## Practical over theoretical

We write for teams that have to ship. An article should leave the reader with something they can do: a rollout order, a checklist, a configuration change, a decision framework. We say when advice depends on team size, budget or risk tolerance, and we never imply that a configuration makes a system completely secure.

## Primary sources first

We build articles on the most direct source available, in roughly this order:

1. The vendor's or project's own advisory, documentation, specification or source code.
2. Government cybersecurity agencies (such as CISA, NCSC and NIST) and standards bodies (such as IETF, OWASP and OASIS).
3. CVE records and machine-readable data such as the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and FIRST EPSS.
4. Original research from security researchers and vendors.
5. Established technology and security publications, used for corroboration or when nothing more direct exists.

When we rely on a secondary report, a vendor's own marketing or a single source, the article says so.

## How we verify

- Every factual claim that matters to a security decision (affected and fixed versions, CVE identifiers, scores, dates, defaults, configuration behavior) is checked against its source before publication.
- Claims we could only partly confirm are qualified in the text ("according to…", "as of…"). Claims we could not confirm are left out.
- Articles end with a **Sources and verification** table that shows what was checked against what, and notes anything that could not be read directly.
- Figures that change daily, such as EPSS scores or KEV counts, are dated.
- We do not invent quotes, statistics, test results or vulnerability details, and we do not claim hands-on testing that did not happen.

## Code and configuration examples

Examples state the language, tool or platform and the versions they assume. We say whether an example was run (and how) or is illustrative only. Commands that change systems are written to be read before they are run.

## News and advisories

We publish on a vulnerability only when there is a primary record of it, such as a vendor advisory, a CVE record or a KEV entry. We separate what the vendor has confirmed from what researchers or other outlets report. For flaws without a fix, we update the article when the vendor publishes one.

## Independence

Our editorial decisions are ours. No company can pay to be covered, to be ranked higher in a comparison, or to review or change an article before it is published. Tool comparisons are based on documented features, pricing and trade-offs, and we say when a comparison is based on documentation rather than our own use.

## Commercial content and affiliate links

- **Sponsored content** is labeled as sponsored at the top of the article, before the reader reaches the content. See [Advertise](/advertise/).
- **Affiliate links**, if an article contains any, are disclosed at the top of that article. A commission never decides whether a product is recommended.
- Any article placed in exchange for payment is sponsored content and is labeled the same way, whoever wrote it.

## Dating and updates

Articles show their publication date and, when changed, an updated date. Security advice ages: tools change, vendors change defaults, new attacks appear. When something in an article stops being true in a way that matters, we update it and say so.

## Corrections

We correct errors when we find them or when readers point them out. Significant corrections are noted in the article and listed publicly on the [corrections page](/corrections/).

## Guest contributions

We publish guest articles from practitioners. Guest posts meet the same sourcing and verification standards as our own articles, are edited and fact-checked by us, and carry the writer's byline. See [Write for Us](/write-for-us/).
